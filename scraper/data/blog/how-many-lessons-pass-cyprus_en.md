You are about to book your first lesson, and one question keeps coming back. How many lessons will this take? Every school answers a little differently, and that is fair, because every learner is different. This guide helps you work out your own number. It also shows how that number turns into a budget you can plan around.

## Is there a fixed number of driving lessons in Cyprus?

No single figure fits everyone. You will hear numbers from friends, from forums and from adverts, but they describe other people. Your own number depends on you.

Here is what we know for sure. Before any lesson, you need your learner licence, known in Greek as the ekpedeftiki adeia. It comes from the Department of Road Transport, called TOM in Cyprus. The [Cyprus provisional driving licence guide](/en/blog/cyprus-provisional-driving-licence) explains how to get it. After that, you learn at a licensed driving school. You sit in a dual-control car with a qualified instructor next to you.

Whether any minimum number of lessons applies is a question of rules. We do not quote a figure here, because rules and fees can change. Ask your school before you pay, and check with TOM if you want to be certain.

Even if a minimum exists, it does not tell you when you are ready. A minimum is a floor. It is not a target. Some learners pass that floor and still need more time. Others feel ready sooner but must wait for the instructor to agree.

So think about lessons in a different way. They are not a number to complete. They are the time you need to reach a standard. The examiner wants to see safe, calm and independent driving. Once you can do that, you have taken enough lessons. Until then, you have not.

The rest of this guide shows you how to judge that standard for yourself.

## What decides how many lessons you personally need?

Several things push your number up or down. Most of them you can judge honestly before the first lesson.

Look at this list and be straight with yourself:

- **Your starting point.** Have you ever driven before, even a little? A complete beginner has more to learn than someone who held a licence years ago.
- **Where you learned.** Cyprus drives on the left. If you learned on the right, your habits will fight you at first, especially at junctions and roundabouts.
- **Your confidence.** Nervous learners often need more time to feel calm. That is normal and nothing to be ashamed of.
- **Your age and reaction habits.** Some people pick up hazard awareness quickly. Others build it slowly.
- **The language of the lesson.** If English is your main language, ask whether your instructor can teach in English. Clear instructions speed up progress.
- **Where you practise.** Busy city traffic and large roundabouts take longer to master than quiet streets.

Two learners with the same starting point can still need different amounts of time. One may practise often and keep every skill fresh. The other may take a lesson once a month and forget half of it each time.

Your instructor sees all of this from the passenger seat. They know how quickly you improve and where you slip. That is why their view matters more than any average. A good instructor tells you plainly what to work on.

Finally, think about your own timetable. Learners who can book lessons close together tend to feel progress sooner. If work or study limits you, plan for a longer stretch instead of a faster one.

If something in your life makes learning harder, say so early. Tell your instructor if you are very nervous. Tell them if you struggle with directions or with left-hand traffic. They can then plan lessons around it.

## How can you estimate your own number before you start?

You cannot know your exact number on day one. You can still build a sensible estimate and improve it as you go.

Follow these steps:

1. Place yourself in one of three groups. You are a complete beginner, a returning driver or an experienced driver from another country.
2. Take your first one or two lessons before you commit to a number. The instructor needs to see you drive.
3. Ask for an honest range after those lessons. A range is better than a promise.
4. Review the estimate every few lessons. It will move up or down as you improve.
5. Keep your own notes. Write down what went wrong and what felt easy.

Complete beginners usually need the most time, because everything is new. Controls, observation and road rules all arrive at once. Returning drivers often move faster on the basics. They still need time to rebuild habits.

Experienced drivers from other countries often pick up the controls quickly. Their main job is to adjust to local roads. That means left-hand traffic and Cyprus roundabouts. Those habits take real practice, even for confident drivers.

Be careful with any number a friend gives you. It describes their start, their nerves and their schedule. Treat it as a rough range that may not fit you.

Also keep the end goal in mind. The [Cyprus driving test guide](/en/blog/cyprus-driving-test-2026) shows what the examiner expects. Read it early, and use it as your finish line. Your estimate is really a guess at how long it takes you to reach that standard.

## What do the learning stages look like from first lesson to test day?

Most learners move through the same stages. The speed differs, but the order stays the same.

**Stage one is controls and basics.** You learn to move off, stop and steer smoothly. Mirror checks start here. You will feel clumsy, and that is expected.

**Stage two is quiet roads.** You practise junctions, signals and speed control away from heavy traffic. You begin to think ahead instead of reacting late.

**Stage three is busy traffic.** Now you meet roundabouts, lane changes and fast-moving roads. This stage often takes the longest, because it asks for quick decisions.

**Stage four is the mock test.** Your instructor sets a route and asks you to drive without prompts. This is the best sign of progress you will get.

**Stage five is test ready.** You drive calmly and safely, and your instructor agrees you are ready.

You may move through stages one and two quickly and then slow down at stage three. That is normal. Busy traffic is where most learners spend extra time. Do not read it as a sign that you are failing.

Write down the stage you are in after every lesson. A short note is enough. After a few lessons the notes show you whether you are moving forward or standing still. They also give you something concrete to discuss with your instructor.

## How do you know you are ready for the test?

Readiness is a pattern, not a feeling. One good lesson does not prove it. Several lessons in a row do.

Look for these signs:

- You drive a full route without your instructor prompting you.
- Mirror, signal, manoeuvre has become a habit, not a thought.
- You handle roundabouts and junctions calmly and give way correctly.
- You spot hazards early and react in good time.
- You keep your lane position and stay within the speed limit.
- You stay calm after a small mistake and carry on safely.
- Your instructor tells you that you are ready.

Be careful with the last point. Your instructor judges readiness from what they have seen. If you feel unsure, say so. Ask for a mock test over a fresh route you do not know.

Do not book a test just because the lesson count feels long enough. A test is not a reward for time served. It is an assessment of how you drive today.

## How do lesson length, frequency and packages change the total?

The number of lessons is only half of the picture. What a lesson means matters just as much.

Ask your school how long one lesson lasts. Some schools count a lesson in minutes, others in hours. If you see a price for a number of hours, check what one hour includes. Take the question "how much is 40 hours of lessons". It has no answer until you know the price and the lesson length.

Frequency matters too. Lessons spread far apart cost you progress. You forget skills and spend the start of each lesson recovering them. Lessons in a regular block help you build habits faster. That can lower the number you need.

Packages bundle lessons at one price. They can be good value if you use every lesson. Before you buy, ask these questions:

- How many lessons are in the package, and how long is each one?
- Does the package expire?
- What happens to unused lessons if you pass early or switch school?
- Is anything else included, such as theory help?

If a school advertises a short block, for example a five hour class, ask what it covers. Ask who it suits. A short block may help a returning driver brush up. It may not be enough for a complete beginner.

## How do you turn your lesson count into a budget?

Once you have an estimate, the maths is simple. You multiply, then you add the extras.

Start with this idea: lesson spend equals the number of lessons times your school's current price per lesson. If you buy a package, use the package price instead. Ask the school for its price in writing. Prices change, and a written quote avoids confusion later.

Then add the other costs. They sit outside the lessons, and many learners forget them:

- Learner licence and medical certificate.
- Theory test fee and practical test fee.
- A second practical test if the first one does not go well.
- Extra lessons before a retest.

We do not quote fees in this guide. Check the current amounts with TOM and your school.

Build three numbers, not one. Choose a hopeful lesson count, a likely count from your instructor and a cautious count. Multiply each by your written price. Plan around the middle number. Keep a cushion for the highest one.

For example, say your hopeful count is A, your likely count is B and your cautious count is C. You multiply each by the written price per lesson. That gives you three lesson budgets. Add the same extras to all three. Then compare the totals. The gap between the first and the last tells you how much cushion to keep.

This approach protects you from a nasty surprise. It also stops you from rushing a test just to save money. A failed test often costs more than a few extra lessons.

Learning to drive in Cyprus is a skill, not a fixed course. Your number of lessons is the time you need to reach a safe, confident standard. Estimate it early and watch the signs of readiness. Ask your school for clear prices in writing. Then budget for a range instead of one hopeful figure. That way, you stay calm about the cost and focused on the driving.

## FAQ

**Is there a minimum number of driving lessons I must take in Cyprus?**
This guide does not state a legal minimum, because rules can change. Ask your driving school and the Department of Road Transport if any minimum applies to your category. Even if one exists, it only sets a floor. Your instructor decides when you are ready for the test.

**How many lessons does a complete beginner need?**
There is no fixed figure. A complete beginner usually needs more time than someone who has driven for years, because every skill is new. Ask your instructor for a range after your first one or two lessons. Then review it every few lessons.

**I already drive in another country. Do I need fewer lessons?**
Often you need fewer lessons on the basics, because you know the controls. You still need time to adjust to local roads. Cyprus drives on the left, and roundabouts take practice. Some foreign licences can be exchanged, so check that option first.

**How do I work out what 40 lessons would cost?**
Multiply 40 by the price your school quotes for one lesson. Or use the package price if you buy a bundle. First ask how long one lesson lasts, because a lesson and an hour may not be the same thing. Ask for the price in writing.

**Should I take more lessons than my instructor suggests?**
Do not buy lessons just to feel safe. Do tell your instructor if you feel unsure about something specific. Ask for a mock test over an unfamiliar route. If you still struggle with the same skill, extra lessons on that skill are a sensible choice.
